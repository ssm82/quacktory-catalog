Universal prompt

A cinematic, editorial-tech interface built on a dark modular mosaic of media panels, governed by strict orthogonal geometry, oversized geometric type treated as a compositional object, and slow mask-reveal motion that reads like camera moves in a storyboard.

The design's direction is immersive and confident, registering as a curated gallery wall rather than a utility surface. Its emotional register is contemplative, dramatic, and slightly theatrical, achieved through deep near-black framing between blocks, full-bleed imagery, oversized white type sitting directly on saturated media, and the deliberate contrast between a cool blue ambient tone and one or two warm chromatic poles used as subject anchors. There is no single hero frame; instead, the first viewport reads as a mosaic with hierarchy carried by panel size and saturation, and the hero strategy IS the mosaic itself — a storyboard layout where one central panel is enlarged enough to act as the focal tableau while flanking panels provide atmospheric rhythm.

The color system is anchored by a near-black base in the HSL range of approximately 210 35 8 to 0 0 4, which acts as the universal canvas and the gutter between every media block. The dominant surface tone sits one step lighter than the base, around 215 28 14, used only when a UI surface must lift off the background. Pure white at #FFFFFF is the primary text and chrome color, used aggressively for legibility. The accent system works as a two-pole chromatic device: a cool mid-tone in the cyan-blue band of roughly 200 45 50 to 205 60 65 (functioning as ambient media tone, water, sky, and the cool side of the temperature dialogue) and a saturated warm pole in the red-to-amber range of 355 65 45 to 25 75 55 (used only on the focal subject in the central panel and sparingly elsewhere to avoid competition). There is no global UI accent color — the warm pole is a subject color, not a chrome color, and using it on buttons, links, or hover states would be a violation. A muted support tone in the deep teal-navy range around 220 35 22 may appear as a subtle tonal lift behind small secondary elements. Contrast logic is simple: white on near-black for everything structural, media carrying its own color story inside each tile, and any text that crosses bright mid-tones (caption floating over imagery) requiring either a vignette darkening or a tinted scrim to pass. The defining trick to preserve is that color lives in the imagery, never in the UI — chrome stays achromatic and lets media do the chromatic work.

Typography is a single-family system built on a geometric grotesque sans, used at extreme scale variation. The display cut, set in a weight around 500 to 600 with slightly tight tracking, occupies approximately 35 to 40 percent of the central panel's height and reads as a compositional object rather than as information. Body, nav, and small chrome all use the same family at descending scales. A second face — a transitional or modern serif — may appear as an overlay accent on a single focal tile, where it functions as a quiet editorial interruption against the dominant sans. A monospaced face is reserved for corner-anchored labels, numeric indices, and tag-like metadata, set in uppercase with wide tracking at a small display size relative to viewport (roughly 1.0 to 1.4 percent of viewport height). The hierarchy ladder runs: wordmark or logotype → small uppercase tracked nav → bracketed tag (the `[WORD]` motif, uppercase, tracked, sitting as a thin label) → giant lowercase display → small paragraph or metadata. Heading-to-body ratio is dramatic, around 8:1 to 12:1. Readability is fast at the display level; small nav and corner labels rely on generous tracking and high contrast to compensate for their small size. Numerics and indices are set in the monospaced face, contributing to the data-stamp feeling of the chrome.

Layout and composition follow a modular mosaic / magazine grid as the primary pattern, with a 12-column underlying grid. On wide screens, the first viewport divides into roughly two rows: a top row of two equal panels at approximately 50/50 width split, and a lower row weighted heavier on the center, often 22 / 55 / 22 or 25 / 50 / 25, with the central enlarged panel carrying the dominant media and the oversized display type. Gutters are thin and consistent — typically 8 to 16 pixels at desktop — and they read as the same near-black as the base, so the impression is of media tiles separated by structural negative space rather than by strokes or borders. Outer margins are minimal, giving a claustrophobic, cinematic feel rather than airy minimalism. Asymmetry is intentional but disciplined: corners are not mirrored, the lower row is weighted heavier than the top, and one flanking tile may carry a contrasting temperature (the warm pole) to set up a chromatic dialogue with the central cool-dominant panel. In a second compositional mode, the page is a single full-bleed illustration or photographic scene with a corner-anchored label system — top-left wordmark, top-right wordmark, top-center caption, mid-left and mid-right labels, bottom-center cue — set on a warm or painterly base, where the negative space appears only as soft floor texture and small typographic gutters, and objects intentionally clip the frame to imply a larger world. Negative space is structural, not decorative: black gutters function as both framing and rhythm, and in the full-bleed mode, vignette darkening in the upper corners solves legibility for the wordmarks without breaking the surface treatment.

Visual hierarchy orders the eye as follows. The first hit is the giant display typography inside the central media block — it is the largest typographic element on the page, white, and reads instantly. The second hit is the central media image itself, dominant by scale and saturation, anchored by a single saturated subject in the warm pole. The third hit is the top navigation and wordmark, smaller but persistent at high contrast. The fourth and fifth hits are the flanking media panels, which read as atmospheric rather than focal. In the full-bleed mode, the first hit is the corner wordmarks (immediate, low-effort scanning), the second is the central saturated anchor object placed slightly below the vertical midpoint, the third is the top-center caption, and the fourth is the small bottom-center cue. Emphasis comes from extreme scale contrast, saturation contrast between the cool base and the warm subject, and central or corner-anchored placement — never from color coding of UI elements. Hierarchy can be quieted by reducing the saturation of competing flanking panels (a 10 percent brightness drop is often enough) and strengthened by enlarging or tightening the tracking of the central display type.

The hero treatment, in design-language terms, is a single large panel functioning as a museum tableau, framed by the dark gutter on all sides, with a saturated subject — typically in the warm pole — sitting in the lower-center of the panel and a giant display headline overlapping the lower half of the subject, reading as a composition rather than as a label. Headline role is compositional, not informational; the user absorbs the visual statement before reading the words. Sub-elements include a single bracketed tag in the upper portion of the panel, set small, uppercase, tracked, in white. In the alternative full-bleed mode, the hero is a single illustrative or photographic scene with a small upper-center caption, a small monospaced label system anchored to the corners and mid-edges, and a "discover"-style cue at bottom-center — the scene replaces the panel, but the same compositional logic holds: a saturated focal anchor, quiet typography, and edge-clipped objects that imply a larger world.

Navigation and wayfinding stay minimal: a thin, horizontal, top-aligned bar in uppercase tracked white on near-black, with no background fill, no border, no shadow, and no visual weight beyond typography. The bar reads as a stable reference point and never animates on scroll. Secondary navigation, when present, lives as small uppercase wordmarks anchored to the top-left and top-right corners of the viewport, functioning as portfolio-style index labels rather than a menu. A scrollytelling driver, if used, must be limited to media layers and never to chrome; parallax offset should not exceed 6 to 8 percent and applies only to imagery, not to UI. The bracket-style tag (`[WORD]`) is the consistent micro-system for secondary labels, and the monospaced corner-label system is the consistent micro-system for the full-bleed mode.

Media and imagery carry the entire content story. Photography is flat matte, full-bleed inside each tile, with varied framing — close-up, mid landscape, wide — to create rhythm across the mosaic. A single tile in any composition may carry a text overlay directly on the image; everything else remains untagged. In the alternative mode, the media is a painterly, grainy, illustrative scene with soft cast shadows, vignette darkening in the upper corners, and intentional edge clipping. Real images may sit alongside abstract or illustrated elements, but the system never mixes both within a single composition — choose one mode per scene. No glass, no drop shadows, no grain on the UI itself; any texture belongs inside the imagery. Bloom, duotone, or color grading should be applied within a single tile only, never globally across the mosaic.

Standout solutions worth replicating in any thematic pivot include: a modular mosaic grid where one central panel is enlarged to roughly twice the width of its flanking panels, anchoring a giant display headline as a compositional object; a dual-temperature palette device where one warm subject and one cool ambient environment create automatic focal hierarchy without UI accent color; black gutter framing that turns separation into a structural rhythm rather than a decorative choice; a single bracketed-tag micro-system `[WORD]` used consistently for secondary metadata so all small labels share one voice; a monospaced corner-label system that turns a single full-bleed scene into an editorial spread; and a single mask-reveal animation on the hero display type that echoes a cinematic camera sweep. Each of these is a reusable pattern, not a one-off.

The lessons to learn from this design are: let the imagery carry the color story and keep the chrome achromatic; build hierarchy through scale and saturation rather than through color coding of UI elements; use black negative space structurally as both gutter and frame so the system reads as a gallery wall; pair each display headline with a single bracketed tag so secondary information shares one consistent voice; and commit to a single motion language — mask reveal for hero type, fade-and-translate for everything else — so the system feels authored rather than assembled.

The watch-outs are specific traps to avoid in any extension. Competing chromatic poles of similar saturation will create focal-point crowding; mute the secondary pole or move it lower in the mosaic. Stacked decorative elements at the edges of a full-bleed scene will steal attention from the central anchor; desaturate or push them back. Text floating over bright mid-tones will fail contrast checks; always add a vignette or tinted scrim under any label that crosses saturated media. Active state on nav should never rely on color alone — add underline, weight shift, or position change so reduced-vision users can perceive it. Clickable cues on tiles and corner labels are missing by default; add a subtle scale or brightness lift on hover, capped at 1.02. The mosaic does not survive at mobile width — define a single-column reflow as canonical, retaining hero scale and contrast. Do not animate text during scroll, and never animate blur or filter over large media areas. And the central red-or-warm subject is a subject color, not an accent color; do not reuse it on buttons, links, or hover states.
