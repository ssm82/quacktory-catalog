Universal prompt

One-liner: An editorial, poster-driven interface where a single oversized display headline acts as both content and architectural backdrop, anchored by a centered grayscale image and finished with thin top and bottom metadata rails that read like a publication rather than an app.

Direction: The page reads as a print magazine spread or gallery wall rather than a typical product surface. The mood is assertive, weighty, theatrical, and serious — the kind of page that demands a sustained glance rather than a quick scan, and that treats type as an object on the page rather than a label for one. The hero strategy is full-bleed typographic monument: a single display headline nearly touching both side edges functions as the dominant graphic event, with a centered, slightly grainy grayscale image intersecting the letterforms and two corner anchors flipping to inverse polarity as bookends. There is no product photography, no UI chrome, no gradient, no glow, no glass — just type, paper, ink, and one textured image doing all the work. Every decision flows from that binary commitment.

Color: The palette is strict binary plus a single paper-tinted surface used on body pages. The dominant background reads as crisp off-white at approximately #FFFFFF on full-bleed surfaces, while editorial body sections shift to a warm paper tone near #F2EFE7, giving inner pages a printed-page quality. Ink is near-black at approximately #000000. There is one mid-gray support tone at roughly #666666, but it lives only inside the image well, never on type, never on chrome. Accent is the same #000000 as the ink, used at full saturation on solid letterform blocks and on the inverse-polarity corner anchors. There are no chromatic accents, no gradients, no glows, no transparency layers, no under-glow tricks, no brand-tinted scrims. Contrast is maximum everywhere: black on white on the hero, black on cream on editorial sections, white on black on the corner blocks. The only chromatic departure is the halftone grain inside the central image, which introduces organic mid-grays against the otherwise binary system. The role logic is white for breathing room, black for structure and message, paper cream for editorial body, mid-gray exclusively for image texture. Any new hue added to the system would damage it; the binary commitment must be preserved absolutely.

Typography: The display face is Anton, a very heavy, near-square, slightly condensed grotesque, set at Black weight, almost always uppercase, with very tight tracking. The display headline sits at roughly fifteen to eighteen percent of vertical viewport cap height, spans nearly the full horizontal width, and is allowed to clip the page edges for energy. The body face is Inter, used at Regular and Medium weights at body sizes around sixteen to eighteen pixels with comfortable leading of roughly 1.5 to 1.6, and at much smaller sizes for metadata strips and table values. Hierarchy is built through weight and scale within or across these two families rather than through competing families. A smaller display variant in Anton or in Inter Bold is used for corner anchors and section labels, typically set tracked uppercase at one and a half to two percent letter-spacing. Accent all-caps with mild tracking appear in the top metadata strip, the bottom caption, and section dividers. There is no mono or numeric face in the system. The display-to-body scale ratio is extreme, roughly eight to one or ten to one, which is what makes the headline read as a poster and the body read as marginalia. Tight letter-spacing on display and generous leading on body are non-negotiable.

Layout and Composition: The first screen is divided into three horizontal bands: a thin top utility strip at roughly four to six percent of viewport height, a dominant middle zone housing the headline and central image at roughly eighty to eighty-five percent, and a thin bottom caption strip at roughly four to six percent. Within the middle zone, the display headline spans the full width and the central image occupies a centered band of roughly thirty to forty percent of viewport width on a strict vertical axis. Corner anchors sit at the lower left and lower right of the middle band as inverse-polarity blocks. The underlying grid is a twelve-column structure with the headline aligned across columns one through twelve, the image centered on columns four through nine, and corner anchors pinned to the outermost columns. Negative space is generous around the image but tight along the headline edges — that contrast between openness around the figure and pressure around the type is the central tension of the layout. Asymmetric balance is rejected in favor of strict axial symmetry on the hero, with intentional controlled overflow where the solid letterforms clip beyond the image zone to inject energy. Inner pages use a strict modular grid, typically a triptych of three equal columns or a 4/8 split with a left-aligned metadata table and a right-aligned media strip, separated by hairline rules.

Visual Hierarchy: The eye lands first on the oversized display headline, decoding in under one second through scale and one hundred percent contrast. Second comes the central grayscale image, placed at the optical center and surrounded by the strongest graphic shapes on the page. Third come the corner anchors, which read as secondary through position and inverse polarity. Fourth is the bottom caption, and fifth is the top metadata strip. Hierarchy is enforced purely by scale, position, and solid-versus-empty contrast — never by color, because the palette has no chromatic hierarchy to offer. The speed of decoding is extremely fast, and the page reads as a single object rather than a composition of competing parts.

Hero Treatment: The hero is a single, centered, axial poster tableau. A massive display headline is set in Anton Black, uppercase, with the letterforms acting as architectural backdrop; the same letterforms, in some compositions, are re-asserted as solid black shapes behind or beneath the photographic figure, so the type reads as both message and stage. The central image is a tightly cropped, vertically centered grayscale photograph with a subtle halftone or grain texture, sized to occupy roughly forty to sixty percent of the headline's width. The image sits directly on the background with no frame, no rounded mask, no shadow, no caption inside the frame. The headline and image share a center axis, with deliberate overlap between the lower edge of the type and the upper edge of the figure. Two corner anchors — small, inverse-polarity blocks reading as labels in Anton or Inter Bold uppercase — sit at the lower left and lower right of the hero band, white type on solid black. The framing logic is museum tableau, not product shot: the figure is spotlit by white negative space, never by glow, never by vignette, never by a colored under-light.

Navigation and Wayfinding: Primary navigation is integrated as a thin top utility strip — a single line of uppercase or sentence-case metadata anchored to the left, with a small date or section indicator to the right, intentionally quiet and high in contrast. There is no visible primary CTA button; instead, the system uses a manifesto-style secondary action phrased as a verbal invitation rather than a hard sell, sitting at the bottom right of the hero or inside one of the corner anchor blocks. Secondary navigation on inner pages uses a small horizontal metadata list with thin black hairline rules separating rows, or a left-aligned table of contents that stays at the same restrained weight as the top strip. There is no hamburger, no floating pill, no sticky bottom bar, no progress indicator. On long-scroll content, a minimal sidebar of section labels can act as wayfinding but must never break the typographic hierarchy. Scrollytelling, if used, is driven by subtle fade-and-translate reveals rather than by pinning, scrubbing, or progress bars.

Media and Imagery: Imagery is treated as one event per page, never as decoration. The central image is always grayscale, always tightly cropped, always centered on the vertical axis, and always rendered with a slight halftone or grain texture to feel printed rather than digital. Real photographs are preferred over illustrations; when illustration is necessary it must be high-contrast, monochromatic, and follow the same axial rules. No color photography, no duotone overlays, no vignette, no drop shadow, no rounded masks, no inline captions. On inner pages, a horizontal strip of three small square or portrait photographs — equal in size, equally spaced, separated by thin gutters — acts as a secondary media band, treated as a contact sheet rather than a gallery. Bloom, glow, glass, blur, and CSS-applied grain filters are all avoided; any texture must be baked into the source asset, not painted on by the browser. A single accent image per hero, a single triptych per inner section, never more.

Standout Solutions: First, use solid display letterforms as both content and architectural shape — set a headline so large that the same word reappears behind the image as a solid black block, allowing the photograph to overlap the type and turn the headline into a stage. Second, design the corner anchors as inverse-polarity blocks rather than standard buttons or chips, so the wayfinding reads as monumental signage rather than as UI chrome. Third, integrate the top metadata strip and bottom caption strip as fixed horizontal rails, ensuring every page has the same bookend weight and the same way of declaring its context. Fourth, render central imagery with a halftone or grain texture so even crisp digital photographs feel like print plates. Fifth, on inner pages, use a triptych of three equal grayscale frames as a recurring content module — three equal columns, equal gutters, equal empty top and bottom bands — and treat it as a signature layout pattern. Sixth, when motion is used, restrict it to a single entrance pattern — opacity plus a small translate of eight to twelve pixels, staggered only on headline lines — and apply the same pattern to every subsequent block so the system speaks with one motion voice.

Things to Learn From: Let the subject carry the color story; do not impose a chromatic brand palette on a system that is built on contrast alone. Integrate type as shape, not as decoration, so the headline doubles as the page's structural backbone. Pair every page with a top utility strip and a bottom caption strip so the wayfinding feels like a publication rather than an app. Use a strict axial composition on hero pages and a strict modular grid on body pages, and never mix the two grammars. Restrict motion to a single, slow, sequential reveal and never let animation carry the visual identity.

Watch-Outs: Watch color vibration where solid black letterforms meet the grainy mid-grays of the central image, because that edge can feel harsh and may need a few pixels of negative space to breathe. Watch competing blacks of similar value when the corner anchor blocks sit adjacent to the headline tail; the eye will not know which is primary unless the headline is unambiguously larger. Watch any caption that crosses a mid-gray region of the image, because contrast can collapse and the text becomes unreadable — lift the caption, add a localized scrim, or move it off the image entirely. Watch clickable affordance on corner anchors, since text-only inverse-polarity blocks do not read as interactive; add an underline, an arrow cue, or a clearly demarcated hit zone if they are meant to be tapped. Watch horizontal overflow on the headline at narrow viewports, because the giant type will clip unless the weight is reduced or a wrap-to-two-or-three-line mobile variant is designed. Watch metadata strips that sit below eleven-pixel sizes, which often fail accessibility thresholds even when colors are pure black on white. Watch parallax on the central image: anything beyond ten to twenty pixels of vertical offset will break the museum tableau feel. Watch pinning full sections, which would make a heavy, slow page feel stuck rather than deliberate. Watch chromatic temptation — every new color added will silently dilute the binary system that gives the page its weight.
