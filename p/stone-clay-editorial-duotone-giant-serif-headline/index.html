Universal prompt

A single one-shot editorial graphic rendered as a flat-vector illustrated poster on a saturated mustard ground, built to read as a scrapbook-style listicle header rather than as a navigable interface, where the whole composition can be absorbed in one glance because a giant numeric, a heavy sans display lockup, a script descender, and a small cluster of doodle-annotated objects are all stacked on a single vertical column and bound together by a single saturated accent that repeats in exactly three places.

Direction. Editorial and illustrative, mid-2010s blog-header register. The piece is not trying to be a website; it is trying to be a printed page that happens to live on a screen. The mood is casual, hand-made, slightly student-zine, with a deliberate tension between the rigid geometric weight of a display sans and the wobble of a marker-pen hand. The hero strategy at a high level is a typographic stack: a colossal numeral with a hard-edged colored offset shadow, a heavy all-caps display line, and a script line that softens the lockup into something that feels personal rather than corporate. Everything below the headline is supportive illustration, not primary content.

Color. The palette is five hues, no more, and the entire piece is built on that restraint. A saturated mustard background in the range of #F4C95C anchors everything and acts as the warm ground on which every other element sits. A near-black ink around #1F1F1F carries all type and all line work. A single saturated accent — a burnt orange in the range of #E8743B — is the only color allowed to repeat, and it does so in exactly three roles: as a hard-edged offset shadow behind the colossal numeral, as the stroke around the central cloud-shaped hub, and as the short underline beneath each handwritten annotation. A slightly darker version of the ground, around #F2B33A, fills the interior of the central hub shape so the hub reads as a soft volume rather than as a hole. Cool greys in the #B8B8B8 family with darker bezels are reserved for the bodies of illustrated devices, which keeps the human-made props from competing with the type. The contrast logic is brutal and simple: maximum black-on-yellow type, with the orange accent earning its keep by appearing only where the eye must be guided. There is no under-glow, no gradient, no blur; the depth effect is created entirely by the offset-shadow trick, not by lighting.

Typography. A heavy display sans in the Archivo Black family, set in a Black or 900 weight, dominates the upper third at a display size of roughly 22 to 28 percent of the viewport height. This face is used in three treatments: the giant numeral with the orange offset shadow, an all-caps tracked-tight line immediately below it, and a larger all-caps display line under that, which together form a three-line typographic stack. A second face, a hand-script in the Caveat family at regular weight and roughly 11 to 14 percent of viewport height, sits as the third line of the title block to introduce warmth and break the geometric rigidity of the heavy sans. A third, informal hand-marker face — same family as the script but rendered in a thinner, smaller, more hurried hand — is used exclusively for the small peripheral annotations and for the lettering inside the central hub shape. There is no monospace face. There is no body copy in the traditional sense. The scale ratio between the giant numeral and the script line is roughly 2.4 to 1, and the ratio between the display all-caps line and the script line is roughly 1.8 to 1, which is what makes the lockup feel like a proper hierarchy rather than three competing typefaces. All-caps treatments are tracked slightly tight, never loose; the script line is the only face that is allowed to breathe with extra leading.

Layout and composition. The whole graphic is a single centered column, portrait-oriented at roughly 2:3, with no gutters and no margin grid in the traditional sense. The upper third holds the typographic stack and consumes about 35 percent of the vertical space. The middle third, roughly 15 to 20 percent, is a breath of negative yellow between the type and the illustrated cluster. The lower half, the remaining 45 to 50 percent, holds the illustration zone, where six to nine small flat-vector objects are scattered in a roughly triangular constellation around a central hub shape. Negative space is used as a compositional tool, not as a luxury: the yellow background is allowed to push through between objects so each prop reads as discrete. Asymmetric balance is enforced by weighting the left side of the cluster with two larger objects and the right side with three to four smaller objects, which prevents the constellation from feeling like a perfect ring. There is no underlying grid; the scatter is deliberately informal.

Visual hierarchy. The eye lands first on the giant numeral at the top because of its sheer size and the orange drop-shadow trick. Second, it travels down to the heaviest all-caps display line immediately below the numeral. Third, it catches the smaller all-caps line above the display line, which retroactively labels the numeral. Fourth, it parses the script line as the softener. Fifth, it drops into the illustration zone and finds the central hub shape because of its central position, its outline color, and the arrows pointing into it. Sixth, it reads the handwritten annotations in a loose orbit around the hub. The path is therefore strictly top-to-bottom and center-to-periphery, with no competing secondary focal point.

Hero treatment. There is no web hero in the traditional sense; the hero equivalent is the typographic stack itself, which functions as a magazine cover. The colossal numeral is the only element that attempts depth, and it does so through a single hard-edged offset of saturated orange to the lower right of the black numeral, with no blur, no gradient, and no glow. The headline beneath the numeral acts as a subhead, the script line acts as a tagline, and the illustration cluster acts as a decorative frame. The framing of the central hub is a soft cloud silhouette stroked in the same orange, filled in the slightly darker yellow surface tone, and lettered with the marker hand — this hub is the visual "stage" of the lower half and every arrow in the cluster terminates at it.

Navigation and wayfinding. There is none. The piece is a static graphic, not a UI. No nav bar, no buttons, no menus, no links, no scroll cues. If the composition is repurposed as a clickable surface, the only legitimate interaction surface is the central hub itself, which should be treated as a single primary call to action rather than as a navigation root. Scrollytelling, ghost chrome, and pill strips are all absent and should remain absent in any thematic pivot; the design language is poster-first, not product-first.

Media and imagery. All imagery is flat vector illustration with no photography, no duotone, no grain, and no bloom. Objects are drawn in a slightly cartoonish mid-2010s flat style with minimal shading: a single fill color, a single darker bezel or accent line, and no inner gradient. Real images are forbidden; if a thematic pivot demands a photograph, it must be illustrated instead, or the entire piece's register will collapse. Curved black arrows in a thick uniform stroke weight connect the objects to the central hub, giving the cluster a sense of circulation without dictating a literal sequence. The arrows are not labeled; they are purely gestural.

Standout solutions. The orange offset-shadow on the colossal numeral is the single most reusable trick in the piece: it lifts one piece of type off a flat ground using only a second flat color, with no blur and no gradient, and it works at almost any scale. The central hub shape, stroked in the same accent color, functions as a visual magnet that ties a scattered cluster of small objects into a single composition without any literal grouping rule. The handwritten annotations, each underscored by a short orange stroke, function as a faux sticky-note texture that signals "this is a list, this is editorial" without ever requiring a numbered list to be drawn. The three-line typographic stack of heavy-sans, all-caps, and script creates rhythm from only two typefaces. The portrait 2:3 aspect ratio with a dominant typographic top half and a busy illustrative bottom half is itself a reusable pattern for any editorial listicle or promotional header. The restrained five-hue palette with one single repeat-accent is the structural backbone of the entire piece and is the most important thing to replicate.

Things to learn from. Let one color do the work of three by repeating a single saturated accent in three exact roles — type shadow, hub outline, and annotation underline — so the eye is trained to read that color as a connective tissue. Establish a focal triangle early by giving the upper typographic stack a clear top-to-bottom path and by placing a single hub object at the visual center of the lower illustration zone. Pair a heavy display sans with a hand-script for the title block rather than with a second sans, because the contrast in stroke contrast is what gives the lockup its rhythm. Keep the palette to five hues and never exceed that count, no matter how many objects are illustrated. Use handwritten peripheral annotations as decorative texture rather than as functional labels, so the piece reads as scrapbook rather than as diagram.

Watch-outs. A hard-edged offset shadow without blur will read as a second, separate instance of the numeral when the piece is rendered below roughly 200 pixels tall, so any thumbnail or feed-card use must be tested at small scale. Cropped peripheral objects at the edges imply an aspect ratio that may not survive a square crop, so the safe zone for any repositioning must be defined before the illustration cluster is placed. The handwritten annotations are decorative, not functional; if reused in a UI context they will read as ambiguous menu items, so they must be re-labeled or removed. The high yellow saturation can cause legibility issues for the orange accents on low-contrast displays and in print, and the orange must be sampled against the exact ground yellow before it is reproduced. The illustrated cluster can easily become cluttered if more than nine objects are added, and any thematic pivot must resist the temptation to add more. The script line will break the lockup if set at the same size as the display line, so the script must always be visibly smaller and lighter. The arrows must remain a uniform thick black stroke, or they will compete with the type. The single accent color must never be used as a fill for an object body, because that turns the connective tissue into noise.
